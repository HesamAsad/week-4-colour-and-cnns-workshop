1

Colour

An entire spectrum, collapsed into three numbers.

Light arrives as a spectrum — the sensor reports three numbers

\( I_R = \displaystyle\int I(\lambda)\,S_R(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad I_G = \int I(\lambda)\,S_G(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad I_B = \int I(\lambda)\,S_B(\lambda)\,d\lambda \)
Three integrals throw almost everything away. Two completely different spectra that give the same three numbers are metamers — and your screen relies on this, faking every colour with just three narrow primaries.
Run the peak slider first without commentary and let them watch the green curve (the 3-spike mixture) re-solve itself to keep matching. Then say what is happening: the spike weights are solved so the two spectra integrate to identical X, Y, Z. The three curves in the lower panel are the CIE 1931 2-degree standard observer functions — the same figure that is in the worksheet, and a standardised stand-in for cone sensitivity. Peak of y-bar at 555 nm is why we are most sensitive to green. Push the peak to the far red or far blue and the spike mixture eventually needs a negative weight — the swatch goes hatched. That is a gamut boundary, and it is why a monitor cannot show every colour you can see. If asked "why three and not four?" — because we have three cone types. Some people have four (tetrachromacy); most animals differ. The number 3 is biology, not physics.

Four ways to write down the same three numbers

SpaceAxesGood forWatch out
RGBred, green, blue storage, display — it is what the sensor and screen speak device-dependent; all three channels move together with brightness
HSVhue, saturation, value "select everything reddish" — thresholding by colour hue is an angle, so it wraps; meaningless when saturation ≈ 0
XYZthe standard observer's response device-independent reference; every other space is defined through it not perceptually uniform; Y alone is luminance
L*a*b*lightness, green–red, blue–yellow colour edits and distances — equal steps look equally different a* and b* are unbounded in theory; needs a white point (D65 here)
They hold the same information, re-parameterised. Choose the space in which your question becomes a simple operation on one axis.
Emphasise the last line — this is the whole reason the lecture spends time on colour spaces. "Make this image less red" is three coupled changes in RGB and one subtraction in Lab. The lecture's ordering is the useful one: RGB is what you get, HSV is an attempt at human vocabulary, XYZ is the physical reference standard, Lab is the perceptual one.

One image, four colour spaces

Same picture, four decompositions — then converted straight back.

In RGB every channel looks like a grey version of the photo — they are highly correlated. In HSV and Lab, brightness lives in one channel and colour in the others, which is exactly the separation that makes editing easy.
This is worksheet exercise 1 (the XYZ and Lab conversions), done live. Click through RGB → Lab and ask them to point at where the two birds separate most cleanly: it is the a* channel, green-to-red, where the blue-and-gold macaw and the red macaw sit at opposite ends. Point out the Y channel of XYZ looks like a sensible greyscale — that is because Y is defined as luminance. Compare to X and Z, which look strange on their own. The right-hand figure is the round trip. It looks identical — but it is not bit-identical, which is the next slide but one.

Pick a pixel, read it four ways

Drag from the red macaw to the blue-and-gold one: a* swings from strongly positive to negative while L* barely moves. That single number is "how red versus how green" — which is why the exercise inverts it.
It opens on the red macaw's plumage. The single most useful drag is from there to the green crown of the blue-and-gold bird — measured on this image, a* goes from +59 to −17, a swing of 76, while L* moves only from 52 to 58. Colour changed enormously; lightness barely moved. That is the separation Lab is for. Other good spots to call out: the gold breast (b* ≈ +78, a* ≈ +6 — yellow is almost purely a b* excursion), the background leaves (a* ≈ −25), and the pale face patch (high L*, small a* and b*). Watch hue on a near-grey pixel — it jumps around wildly, because hue is undefined when saturation goes to zero. Worth saying out loud: that instability is a real source of bugs when people threshold on hue.

What the a* and b* axes actually are

Neutral grey sits at a* = b* = 0, in the centre. "Invert the a* axis" means reflect this plane left-to-right: every red becomes its matching green, and greys are left untouched.
Drag L* up and down and point at the faded region — those are Lab colours with no sRGB equivalent. Lab is bigger than any display gamut, which is a feature: it can describe colours your screen cannot show. The centre-is-grey fact is the key to the next slide. Because grey is the fixed point, inverting a* leaves the lighting and the neutral parts of the scene alone, and only re-colours what was coloured.

Two ways to "swap the colours" — and why they differ

Swapping R and G also moves brightness around, because both channels carry luminance. Inverting a* changes only the green–red opponent axis: L* is untouched, so the shading and the shadows survive intact.
This is exactly the "Example: colour swap" slide from the Image Formation II lecture, and worksheet exercise 2. Get them to look at the white beak and the dark shadows: in the RGB swap the tones shift, in the Lab flip they do not. Ask: which one would you use to recolour a product photo without making it look badly lit? That is the argument for perceptual spaces in one question. Note the code shown does 255 − a on the uint8 array. The solution notebook writes -img_lab[:,:,1] instead, which relies on unsigned wrap-around — see the gotchas slide.

Four things OpenCV will do to you

1 · IMREAD GIVES YOU BGR

plt.imshow expects RGB, so a raw imshow(img) shows the birds with red and blue traded.
rgb = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB)

2 · uint8 RANGES ARE SQUASHED

Hue is 0–360° but a byte only holds 0–255, so OpenCV stores H/2 → 0–179. Lab stores L×255/100 and a*, b* offset by +128, so neutral grey is 128, not 0.

3 · BGR2XYZ SKIPS THE GAMMA

It applies the XYZ matrix to the gamma-encoded values. BGR2Lab linearises first; BGR2XYZ does not — so the two are not consistent with each other.
For mid-red (128,0,0): OpenCV gives X = 53, the gamma-correct answer is X ≈ 23.

4 · ROUND TRIPS ARE NOT LOSSLESS

Every conversion re-quantises to 8 bits. On kodim23.png, converting there and back changes ~75–80% of pixels:
max |error| — HSV 5, Lab 8, XYZ 24. Convert to float first if you plan several hops.
EMPHASISE: point 4 is the one that gets marks. The worksheet titles a figure "hsv converted image" and it looks identical — it is not. Students should form the habit of measuring: np.max(np.abs(a.astype(int) - b.astype(int))) — the same instrument as last week. The numbers on this slide were measured on kodim23.png, not remembered — you can rerun them in one cell if a student challenges you. Point 3 is genuinely an inconsistency inside OpenCV, not a student mistake. If someone compares cv2's XYZ against a colour-science library and gets different answers, this is why. Do not let them conclude their code is broken. Point 2 explains a very common bug: students write mask = (hsv[:,:,0] > 300) to select magenta and get an empty mask, because hue never exceeds 179. A fifth one worth mentioning verbally: the solution notebook inverts a* with -img_lab[:,:,1] on a uint8 array. That is modular arithmetic — it computes 256 − a, which happens to be exactly right, because stored = a* + 128. It is correct but accidental-looking; 255 − a is off by one, and doing it in int then clipping is what you would actually write.

Part 1 in one line each

  • Colour is three integrals of a spectrum, not three properties of a surface.
  • Because it is three integrals, different spectra can look identical — metamerism is what makes displays possible.
  • Colour spaces are re-parameterisations: same information, different axes.
  • Pick the space where your edit is one operation on one axis.
  • Every conversion is lossy in 8 bits. Measure, don't eyeball.
Next: that spectrum did not come from nowhere. It is a light source, bounced off a surface, at an angle — and all three of those are baked into every pixel you have.
2

Light and surfaces

Where a pixel value actually comes from.

Diffuse (Lambertian) reflectance

\[ {\color{#7fd6a8} I_D(x)} \;=\; {\color{#f7d774} I_L} \;\cdot\; {\color{#f2a65a} R} \;\cdot\; \bigl(\mathbf{N}(x) \cdot \mathbf{L}\bigr) \]
A matte surface scatters light equally in all directions, so where you stand doesn't matter — only how the surface is tilted relative to the light. That single dot product is the entire shading model.
Drag the direction slider and let them watch the terminator sweep across the sphere. Then make the point that the camera never moves in this demo and would not matter if it did — that is what "diffuse" means, and it is why Lambertian is the easy case. Turn R down and I_L up so the sphere returns to the same brightness. Ask: has anything about the object changed? Yes — but not in the image. Hold that thought for two slides. The warm/blue light buttons matter for part 1: the light's own colour multiplies the surface's reflectance, which is why white paper looks warm under a tungsten bulb, and why "what colour is this surface" is not a question the image can answer directly. Note N·L is clamped at zero — a surface facing away receives nothing. That clamp is the only non-linear thing here, and it is what creates the sharp terminator.

So what sets the R, G, B of one pixel?

\( I_L \) — THE LIGHT

Its intensity and its colour. A red surface under blue light is not red in the image.

\( R \) — THE SURFACE

Reflectance, or albedo: the fraction of each wavelength the material sends back. This is the thing we usually mean by "the object's colour".

\( \mathbf{N}\cdot\mathbf{L} \) — THE GEOMETRY

The angle between the surface normal and the direction to the light. Pure shape, no colour.
This is part (i) of the worksheet exercise. Three things — light, material, orientation — and the image only ever shows you their product.
Make them say the three out loud before you reveal the cards. The marks in the worksheet are for naming all three, and students routinely give two: they forget the light's colour, or they forget the angle. Say explicitly that "colour of the light source" and "intensity of the light source" are one parameter here (I_L is a per-channel intensity), so listing them separately is fine too. Not on the list, deliberately: the camera position. For a Lambertian surface it genuinely does not appear in the equation. For a shiny surface it would — that is the specular case, two slides on.

The problem: only the product reaches the camera

A bright surface in dim light and a dark surface in bright light produce the identical image. Recovering the world from one picture is underconstrained — there are more unknowns than measurements.
Slide it slowly and let the middle figure stay visibly constant. The point lands harder if nobody says anything for a few seconds. This is the lecture's "goal of vision" slide restated as a problem: we want R and N, we measure only I_L · R · (N·L). Three unknowns, one number per channel. Every classical solution adds an assumption to close the gap: constant albedo (shape from shading), smooth normals, "lighting is smooth so sharp edges must be reflectance", known boundary normals. None works in general — that is the honest summary in the lecture. Modern answer: a network learns a prior over scenes from data instead of you writing one down. Same underconstrained problem, different source of the extra assumption. That is a nice one-sentence bridge to part 3 if you want it early.

Your visual system solves it anyway — by guessing well

\( \text{Luminance} \;=\; {\color{#f2a65a}\text{Reflectance}} \;\times\; {\color{#f7d774}\text{Illumination}} \)

WHAT YOU SEE

In Adelson's checker-shadow figure, two squares with identical pixel values look obviously different — because your visual system has already decided one of them is in shadow and is reporting reflectance, not brightness.

WHAT THAT COSTS

The split is a guess built on assumptions: illumination varies smoothly, shadows have soft edges, surfaces are locally uniform. Break the assumptions and the guess breaks — which is exactly what a visual illusion is.
"Lightness constancy" is the visual system doing an inverse problem with a prior. The illusion is not a bug — it is the price of the prior being usually right.
If you have a projector and 20 spare seconds, pull up persci.mit.edu/gallery/checkershadow live and cover the surround with two fingers so the two squares abut — they visibly become the same grey. It is the single most convincing demo in this part of the subject, and it is Adelson's own page (he is the source cited in the lecture slides). Link it back: this is the same decomposition as the lecture's "recovering surface reflectance" figure — an image factored into a reflectance layer and an illumination layer. Intrinsic image decomposition is still an active research area. Do not let this become a psychology tangent. The point for this subject is: the problem is underconstrained, so any solution — biological or learned — is importing assumptions.

Your turn: which parameter is changing?

All surfaces Lambertian, one light source. At the circle — what is changing, and what is constant?

Do NOT press reveal until they have committed to an answer. Take a show of hands per image. Image 1 — illumination changes. Same square, same wood, flat board: R and N are constant. Image 2 — reflectance changes. Crossing from a light square to a dark one on the same flat, evenly lit board. Image 3 — reflectance AND the surface normal change; the light source is constant. The arrow leaves the flat board and climbs the curved base of the piece. The common wrong answer on image 3 is "reflectance only" — students see the colour change and stop. Push them: is the base of that piece facing the same way as the board? No. So N changed too, and you can see it in the gradient up the curve. The point of the whole exercise: a change in the image tells you something in the world changed, but NOT which of the three it was. That ambiguity is why the next slide matters.

Which is why vision leans so hard on edges

THE ARGUMENT

We cannot recover \(R\) or \(\mathbf{N}\) from one image. But a change in \(I_D\) reliably signals a change in something real: an occlusion boundary, a corner, a change of material, or a shadow.
Which is why last week's derivative filters are not a side topic — they are the most robust measurement we have.

AND THEY ARE INVARIANT

∂I/∂x is invariant to an intensity shift \(I + b\) and tolerant to a contrast change \(aI\). Turn the room lights up and the edges stay put.
Invariant to X = does not vary with X. Tolerant to X = mostly insensitive to X. The lecture's distinction, and it is examinable.
Almost every vision system starts by measuring local change — and, as we are about to see, a CNN rediscovers exactly that in its first layer without being told to.
This slide is the hinge of the whole workshop. Say the punchline slowly: we are about to train a network that was given no hint about edges, and its first-layer filters will come out looking like the derivative filters we hand-designed last week. The invariance list from the lecture is worth running verbally: translation (yes, for a convolution), rotation (no, for an oriented filter), scale (no — a corner stays a corner but an edge's blur changes), 3D pose (no). Ask which of those a CNN gets for free. Answer: translation, and only translation — the rest has to come from data augmentation or architecture. If time is short, this slide can be delivered in 60 seconds and still do its job.

The same convolution — with the numbers chosen by data.

Last week we chose the numbers. This week we don't.

The convolution operation does not change at all. What changes is where the 25 numbers in each kernel come from.
Land this hard. Students arrive thinking a CNN is a new kind of mathematics; it is last week's arithmetic with an optimiser attached. One honest caveat, worth saying once: Keras's Conv2D actually computes cross-correlation, not convolution — the same thing cv2.filter2D does, and the same distinction we drew last week. Since the weights are learned, the flip is absorbed into the learned values and makes no practical difference. The name is a historical misnomer everyone lives with.

The dataset: MNIST

60,000 training and 10,000 test images, 28×28, one 8-bit channel, labels 0–9 — and roughly 6,000 examples per class, so accuracy is a fair metric here. On an unbalanced dataset it would not be.
The balance point is worth 30 seconds: the worksheet plots the class histogram for a reason. If one class were 90% of the data, a model that always guessed it would score 90% and be useless. Get them in the habit of checking the class distribution before trusting accuracy. A caution about MNIST itself: it is solved. 98–99% is unremarkable, and the gap between a good and a bad model here is a fraction of a percent. It is a teaching dataset, not a benchmark. Do not let them conclude their architecture choices are validated by MNIST. cmap='binary' is why the digits are black on white — the raw data is white on black.

Why divide by 255?

Gradient descent takes one step size in every direction at once. If features live on wildly different scales, the loss surface is a stretched valley and no single learning rate suits it.
Press the button. The contours go round and the path stops zig-zagging. Be precise about what the picture is: axes are two weights, contours are the loss, the orange line is the descent path. The stretch factor is the ratio of the input scales. For MNIST specifically the effect is milder than the picture suggests — all 784 inputs share the same 0–255 range, so they are already commensurate with each other. The real problems with unscaled inputs here are that the initial activations and gradients are ~255× larger than the initialisation assumes, so Adam's first steps are badly mis-scaled. The lesson generalises to any model where features have genuinely different units. Do not oversell this. astype(float)/255 is one line and always safe; that is the whole practical point.

An MLP starts by throwing the geometry away

Flatten() turns 28×28 into a list of 784 numbers with no record of which were neighbours. A fully-connected layer treats pixel (0,0) and pixel (27,27) as exactly as related as two adjacent pixels — which is to say, not related at all.
The shuffle argument is the cleanest way to make this concrete, and it is a real result: permute every image with the same fixed permutation and an MLP's achievable accuracy is unchanged, because a permutation of the inputs can be undone by permuting the first layer's weights. A conv net collapses, because its whole design assumes neighbours mean something. So a CNN's advantage is not extra capacity — it is a *constraint*, a prior that says nearby pixels are related and the same pattern is worth looking for everywhere. Constraints that are true about your data are free accuracy. If someone asks whether the MLP could learn the spatial structure from data: in principle yes, with enough data; in practice it wastes capacity rediscovering something you already knew.

A dense layer's weights are (inputs × outputs), plus one bias per output. Nearly every parameter is in the first layer — because it has to look at all 784 pixels at once.
Make them compute 784×16+16 before you show it. The worksheet explicitly asks them to do this by hand, and the bias term is what people forget. Ask: what would happen to the count if we used 128 hidden units instead of 16? 784×128+128 = 100,480 — eight times the parameters. Then ask whether that would help. On MNIST, a little. The point is that scaling an MLP means paying linearly in the input size, every time. Test accuracy for this model in the solution notebook is about 95.4%. The two non-linearities in this model

ReLU sits after each hidden layer: without something non-linear between them, stacked linear layers collapse into a single linear layer. Softmax sits at the very end, turning 10 unconstrained scores into a distribution.
The "collapse" argument is worth stating as algebra: W2(W1 x) = (W2 W1) x, which is just another matrix. Depth without non-linearity buys nothing. That single line justifies every activation function in the subject. Drag the scale slider: multiplying the scores by a larger number makes softmax more confident, by a smaller one more uniform. Nothing about the *ranking* changes — argmax is unaffected. So a confident-looking probability is not evidence of a correct answer. Connect to the loss: because the output is a probability distribution and the labels are integers, the loss is SparseCategoricalCrossentropy with from_logits=False — "sparse" because labels are 3, not [0,0,0,1,0,...]. If they one-hot the labels themselves, they must switch to CategoricalCrossentropy.

A conv layer is last week's sliding window

One kernel sweeps the whole image and produces one activation map (lectures 6–8's term; worksheet 5 calls the same thing a feature map). The same 25 weights are reused at every position — that reuse is called parameter sharing, and it is where the savings come from.
These are the real trained filters from the network we run in a few slides — click through filters 0 to 7 and note that they genuinely differ: some respond to strokes at one orientation, some to blobs, some are near-dead. Two things to say while it sweeps: 1. The kernel never changes as it moves. That is what makes the layer translation-equivariant: shift the digit, and the activation map shifts with it. 2. 8 filters means 8 of these maps, computed independently and stacked into a 24×24×8 volume. Ask why the output is 24 and not 28 — that is the next slide, and it is exactly the "valid" convolution from last week, where we only keep positions at which the kernel fits fully.

Lecture 7 writes the no-padding case as \( \lceil (W - F + 1)/S \rceil \) and the padded case as \( \lceil W/S \rceil \). The first is algebraically the same formula as the one above — use whichever you find easier to remember.
Make them do the arithmetic for at least one row before revealing it — this is exactly the kind of question that appears on the exam and in next week's worksheet. On the two forms: lecture 7 (CNNs II) gives ceil((W − F + 1)/S) for the no-padding case, which is the same number as floor((W − F)/S) + 1 for every W, F and S — write n = W − F as qS + r and both come to q + 1. Students sometimes think they have two competing rules; they have one. The CS231n form generalises more readably to non-zero padding, which is why it is on the slide. The floor only matters when the stride does not divide evenly; here it never does anything. Say so, so they do not think it is decorative. Common student error: forgetting that pooling shrinks by exactly a factor of 2 only because the pool size equals the stride. Keras's MaxPooling2D defaults strides=pool_size, which is why (2,2) gives non-overlapping blocks — the worksheet's table spells this out.

Max pooling: keep the strongest response, forget where exactly

It has no parameters. It buys a little tolerance to small shifts, and it shrinks every later layer's work by 4×.
Emphasise "no parameters" — students often expect pooling to be learned. It is a fixed reduction, which is why it appears with 0 in the summary output. Lecture 7 puts max pooling alongside two alternatives, worth naming here so the worksheet connects: strided convolution (cheapest, but the window skips over parts of the image) and average pooling (blurs over features, so strong responses get diluted). Max pooling is the one most likely to preserve the strongest feature, at the cost of being the slowest of the three. Note also that "tolerance to small shifts" is the lecture's careful wording — pooling does not buy true invariance. The trade: throwing away three quarters of the positions is exactly downsampling, and last week we spent a whole session on why downsampling without pre-filtering causes aliasing. Max pooling is not a low-pass filter. If you have 30 seconds, mention Zhang (ICML 2019), "Making Convolutional Networks Shift-Invariant Again" — blur before the stride and CNNs get measurably more shift-stable. Same argument as Assignment 1, applied inside a network. That link back to Week 3 is the most valuable thing on this slide.

What it actually learned

Several are clear oriented light-to-dark ramps — last week's derivative filters, discovered rather than designed. But look again: unlike Sobel, most of these sum well above zero, so they measure "how much ink is here" and "which way it runs" at the same time.
This is the payoff for the "why edges survive" slide in part 2. Say it explicitly: we argued from physics that local change is the robust thing to measure, and a network given nothing but pixels and labels partly rediscovered it. But do NOT oversell the resemblance — the honest reading is the interesting one. Measured on these actual weights: filters 0, 3 and 7 have strong directional gradients and really do look like oriented derivative operators. The other five are weaker and more diffuse. And every one of the eight has a positive weight sum, between +0.34 and +2.39, where a Sobel kernel sums to exactly zero. That difference is the lesson. A hand-designed edge filter is built to ignore absolute brightness. This network was never asked to; on MNIST, "is there ink in this patch" is a genuinely useful feature, so it learned filters that report both. Learned features answer the task you set, not the task in the textbook. All eight are active — none is dead — so do not claim otherwise if a student asks. Also worth flagging: at 5×5 with only 8 filters on MNIST, these are much less clean than the famous Gabor-like filters in AlexNet's first layer on ImageNet. Next week's worksheet visualises VGG16's, which are far more striking. If asked how these were obtained: this exact architecture was trained offline in numpy, and these are its real weights; the next slide runs the real forward pass in the browser.

The whole network, running right now

Sharp, digit-shaped responses early; small, abstract, hard-to-read maps late. That progression — from where the strokes are, to what the strokes mean — is the whole idea of a deep network.
Click through several digits. Ask them to watch the 4×4 maps at the end: they are no longer readable as pictures, but the classifier reads them perfectly. Representations do not have to be human-legible to be useful. Find a digit where the softmax is not near-certain (some of the 8s and 9s) and use it to make the point that the second-place class is usually a visually plausible confusion — the model's mistakes are structured, not random. MLP vs CNN — the answer to the worksheet's last question

MLPCNN

Fewer than half the parameters, and less than half the errors. Not because the CNN is bigger, but because parameter sharing and locality are true of images — so the constraint costs nothing and saves a great deal.
The error-count row is there because "95% vs 98%" sounds like a small gap and "460 errors vs 200" does not. Same numbers, honest framing. Use it. Be precise on the last two rows, because the tempting slogan is wrong: - The CNN's parameters are NOT mostly in the dense layer here — conv 2 is the biggest single block at 3,216 of 5,994 (54%), because each of its 16 filters spans all 8 incoming channels. - "A CNN doesn't care about image size" is only true of the CONV layers. Double the input to 56×56 and the conv layers still hold 208 + 3,216, but the flatten goes from 256 to 1,936, so the final dense jumps from 2,570 to 19,370 — a 3.8× total, barely better than the MLP's 3.96×. That second point is worth the extra thirty seconds: it is exactly why real architectures replace flatten-then-dense with global average pooling, and it is the reason "where do the parameters actually live" is a question worth asking of every model. Note also the CNN is slower per epoch despite having fewer parameters — parameter count and compute are different resources. Students conflate these constantly. Caveat to state plainly: these are single-run numbers on an easy dataset, with different epoch counts (20 for the MLP, 10 for the CNN). The direction of the result is robust; the exact figures are not, and their own runs will differ by a few tenths of a percent.

What to carry out of this room

Habits that outlast the worksheet.

Take-homes — colour and light

1 · CHOOSE THE SPACE, THEN THE OPERATION

Before writing a colour manipulation, ask which axis of which space your intent is a single move along. If the answer is "three coupled changes in RGB", you are in the wrong space.
"Keep the lighting, change the hue" is one subtraction in Lab and a mess in RGB.

2 · A CONVERSION IS A COMPUTATION, NOT A RELABELLING

Every hop through cvtColor quantises, clips, and may or may not linearise. Convert to float once, do all your work, convert back once.
And check the ranges — the one that bites is hue at 0–179.

3 · A PIXEL IS A PRODUCT, NOT A PROPERTY

\(I_D = I_L \cdot R \cdot (\mathbf{N}\cdot\mathbf{L})\). When something in an image changes, the useful question is not "what colour is that" but which of the three moved?
Light, material, orientation. Getting into the habit of listing all three is most of the marks on this kind of question.

4 · UNDERCONSTRAINED MEANS YOU MUST ADD SOMETHING

One image, three unknowns. Every method that recovers shape or reflectance is smuggling in an assumption — smooth lighting, constant albedo, a learned prior.
Name the assumption out loud and you will predict where the method fails.
EMPHASISE card 4. It is the single most transferable idea in this half of the subject, and it reappears in stereo, in optical flow, and in every generative model later in the course. "What extra assumption is this method making?" is a question that never stops being useful. Card 3 is the exam-technique one. Students lose marks by answering "the surface is darker" when the question asked which parameters changed.

Take-homes — learning

5 · COUNT THE PARAMETERS BEFORE YOU TRAIN

(F·F·Cin)·Cout + Cout for conv, in·out + out for dense. It takes ten seconds and tells you where your model's capacity actually is.
Do the sum rather than guessing: in today's CNN it is the second conv layer that dominates, not the dense one — and that only flips as the input grows.

6 · AN ARCHITECTURE IS A SET OF ASSUMPTIONS

Convolution assumes nearby pixels are related and that a useful pattern is worth finding everywhere. Those are claims about images, and they are why the CNN wins with fewer parameters.
When an architecture underperforms, ask which of its assumptions your data violates.

7 · ACCURACY IS NOT A RESULT ON ITS OWN

Report it against a baseline, on a balanced set, from more than one run — and prefer error counts when the numbers are close to 100%.
95% vs 98% sounds marginal. 460 errors vs 200 does not. Same data.

8 · TRAIN, VALIDATION AND TEST ARE THREE DIFFERENT THINGS

validation_split=0.2 carves the validation set out of the training data. It guides your choices; it does not measure them.
The moment you tune anything against the test set, it stops being a test set.
EMPHASISE card 8. It is the one with real consequences for the assignment and the project. Say plainly: if you pick your epoch count by looking at test accuracy, your reported test accuracy is optimistically biased and no longer means what you claim it means. Card 5 connects directly to next week's worksheet, which opens with a parameter-counting exercise on a larger network. If they can do the two counts on today's slides, that question is free marks. Card 6 is the conceptual bridge to everything after this: transformers relax the locality assumption, and pay for it with data. Deliberately NOT covered today, because the worksheet does not use them, but flag that they are coming so nobody thinks this model is complete: regularisation (L1/L2, dropout, early stopping) and data augmentation, both in lecture 7, and transfer learning in lecture 8. Our CNN has no regularisation at all — it does not need it on MNIST, and that is itself worth saying, because the lectures introduce regularisation as the response to overfitting on harder datasets.

The five exercises

1 · RGB → XYZ AND RGB → Lab

Convert, show each channel, convert back.
Show channels with cmap='gray' — they are single-channel, not colour.

2 · INVERT THE a* AXIS

Convert to Lab, flip a, convert back.
Neutral is stored at 128, not 0 — so the flip is 255 − a, not −a on a raw uint8. Compare against the R↔G swap.

3 · WHICH PARAMETER IS CHANGING?

The three chessboard images: name the parameters, then say which move and which hold.
Part (i) wants all three named. Part (ii) wants a decision per image — and image 3 has two answers.

4 · RESHAPE FOR KERAS

Make the input 4-D: (N, 28, 28, 1).
np.expand_dims(x, axis=-1). Do this before the visualisation cell — it indexes [s,:,:,0] and will fail otherwise.

Fill in the four missing layers, train it, then answer: how does it compare with the MLP on parameter count and test accuracy?

Heads-up on the notebook: the exercises restart their numbering halfway through (there are two "Exercise 1"s and two "Exercise 2"s), and the shading question is labelled Exercise 5 in your copy but Exercise 3 in the solutions. Go by the section heading, not the number.
Walk the room in this order of likely trouble: Exercise 2 — the uint8 inversion. Many will write -img_lab[:,:,1] because the solution does. That works by modular arithmetic (it computes 256 − a) but it is not what anyone means to write, and it silently misbehaves at a = 0. Get them to do it in int and clip. Exercise 4 — the ordering trap. If they run the visualisation cell before doing the expand_dims, they get an IndexError, and it is not obvious why. Exercise 5 — the layer-2 parameter count. They will write 5*5*16+16 and get 416 instead of 3,216, forgetting the 8 input channels. Also warn them: summary() after training shows a bigger "Total params" than the trainable count, because Keras 3 counts Adam's optimiser state. The number the exercise wants is "Trainable params".
